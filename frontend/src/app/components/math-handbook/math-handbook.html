Chapter 1

Random Walks & the Stock Market

Why prices are stochastic — and what that gives us as traders.
📖 What this chapter is about

Stock prices look chaotic minute-to-minute. But that chaos has a mathematical structure — it's a random walk. This chapter shows you that structure and explains why understanding it unlocks everything else: option prices, probabilities of profit, and risk measurements.

By the end you'll be able to: (a) describe how a stock's "uncertainty cone" grows with time, (b) compute the expected one-month range of any liquid stock, (c) understand why all option pricing rests on this single foundation.

At any moment, a stock's next tick depends on news, flows, and a thousand factors we can't observe. Cumulatively this looks like a random walk — the foundation of every option price you'll ever pay or collect.

Equation 1.1 — discrete random walk

Sn = S₀ + ε · (X₁ + X₂ + ... + Xn), where each Xi ∈ {−1, +1} with probability ½

In trading, you don't need to predict each day; you need to know the shape of where prices can land. Random walks give you that shape.

Equation 1.2 — variance grows linearly with time

Var(Sn) = n · ε²  ⟹  StDev(Sn) = ε · √n
@if (expandedVar() === '1.2') {
Variable definitions
Var(Sn)Variance of the price after n steps How spread out possible prices are; squared units of price.
StDev(Sn)Standard deviation Square root of variance; same units as price (₹).

How it's derived: Each step Xi has variance 1 (since X = ±1 with equal probability, mean 0, variance E[X²] = 1). The total variance after n independent steps is the SUM of variances: n · 1 = n. }
📝 In plain English, this equation says

The variance (a measure of "how spread out" possible prices can be) grows directly proportional to time — wait twice as long, variance doubles. But variance is in squared units. So the standard deviation (variance's square root, in regular units of price) grows only with the square root of time. Wait 4× as long → uncertainty grows 2×, not 4×.

Picture a cone opening from today's price. The cone widens, but it widens slower than a triangle would. That's the √t shape.

With our example: after T = {{ Tdays() }} days, StDev = {{ S() }} · {{ sig() }} · √({{ Tdays() }}/365) = {{ (S() * sig() * Math.sqrt(Tdays()/365)) | number:'1.0-0' }} Nifty points ({{ ((sig() * Math.sqrt(Tdays()/365)) * 100).toFixed(2) }}% of spot).

The dashed red line is the expected price path (grows with μ). The blue band is where prices land 68% of the time (±1σ); the lighter band is the 95% region (±2σ). Notice the shape: the bands widen as √t — fast at first, slower later. Drag σ in the example bar to see the cone fatten or shrink.
💡 Why this matters The √t scaling law. This is why a 30-day option is NOT 30× as risky as a 1-day option — only √30 ≈ 5.5× as risky. Every option premium you've ever paid uses this rule. It's also why "I'll wait one week and see" doesn't reduce your risk by half — it only reduces it by ~30%. Time and risk are not linear partners.
📈 Live: Five random walks
Each path is one possible future. Notice they fan out — variance grows with √t. Higher σ → wider fan. This is why uncertainty is the fundamental input to option pricing.

Why this matters for options

The random walk gives us a probability distribution over where the price could end up. Option pricing reduces to: weight each possible final price by its probability, multiply by the option's payoff at that price, and sum. Without a distribution model, no pricing is possible.

Chapter 2

Log-Normal Distribution

Why we model log returns as Normal — and what falls out.
📖 What this chapter is about

Random walks predict the ±change. But stock prices can never go below zero, and a 10% gain followed by 10% loss isn't 0%. So we need a smarter model. This chapter introduces Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM) — a random walk on the LOG of price. From it we derive the log-normal distribution, which is the engine behind every modern option pricer.

By the end you'll be able to: (a) compute the probability of any future stock price, (b) understand why "expected price" ≠ "most likely price", (c) see why fat upside tails matter for buyers and not for sellers.

If a stock at ₹100 falls 10% then rises 10%, you end at ₹99 — not ₹100. Multiplicative moves don't commute. The fix: model the logarithm of price as a random walk.

This is the single most important transformation in finance. From this Normal distribution, you can compute the probability of ANY price level at expiry.

Basics

ITM, ATM, OTM — The Language of Options

Before pricing, we need vocabulary. Where is the strike relative to the spot?
📖 What this chapter is about

Every option has a strike (K) and at any moment, a spot price (S). The relationship between these two determines whether the option is In-The-Money, At-The-Money, or Out-of-The-Money. This single concept is the vocabulary every options trader must master.

By the end you'll: (a) instantly classify any option as ITM/ATM/OTM, (b) see how each of the four option positions (long/short × call/put) relates to spot, (c) understand which positions WANT spot above strike vs below.

The three states

For any option contract, at any moment:

For CALLS (right to buy at K):
• ITM — Spot > Strike. The right to buy below market is valuable.
• ATM — Spot ≈ Strike. The right is worth roughly its time value.
• OTM — Spot < Strike. The right to buy above market has only hope value.
For PUTS (right to sell at K):
• ITM — Spot < Strike. The right to sell above market is valuable.
• ATM — Spot ≈ Strike.
• OTM — Spot > Strike. The right to sell below market has only hope value.

Visual map — where ITM/OTM zones live

Mirror image: calls and puts have OPPOSITE moneyness zones. A call at K=24,500 is OTM when Nifty is 24,000. A put at the SAME K=24,500 is ITM at Nifty 24,000.

The four positions — who wants what?

Every options trade is one of four positions. Each has a different ITM/OTM preference at expiry:

1️⃣ Long Call (Call Buyer)

Bet: Spot will rise above K + premium.
Wants at expiry: ITM (Spot > K) — and the more, the better.
Max loss: Premium paid (capped).
Max gain: Unlimited.
📐 Long Call payoff

2️⃣ Short Call (Call Seller / Writer)

Bet: Spot will stay below K + premium.
Wants at expiry: OTM (Spot < K) — and the further below, the better.
Max gain: Premium received (capped).
Max loss: Unlimited (rises with spot above K).
📐 Short Call payoff (mirror of long call)
Symmetric: the seller's payoff is the BUYER's payoff flipped vertically. The seller's gain comes only from the premium received; the loss potential is unbounded if spot rallies.

3️⃣ Long Put (Put Buyer)

Bet: Spot will fall below K − premium.
Wants at expiry: ITM (Spot < K) — the further below, the better.
Max loss: Premium paid (capped).
Max gain: K − premium (if spot goes to 0).
📐 Long Put payoff

4️⃣ Short Put (Put Seller / Writer)

Bet: Spot will stay above K − premium.
Wants at expiry: OTM (Spot > K).
Max gain: Premium received.
Max loss: K − premium (if spot goes to 0).
📐 Short Put payoff (mirror of long put)
This is the CSP curve. Notice you collect a flat premium when OTM, and "buy" the underlying at K when ITM.

The four-position cheat sheet

Position Wants spot to be Wants ITM/OTM at expiry? Max risk
Long Call Higher ITM (Spot > K) Premium paid
Short Call Lower or flat OTM (Spot < K) Unlimited
Long Put Lower ITM (Spot < K) Premium paid
Short Put Higher or flat OTM (Spot > K) K − premium (large)

Why moneyness matters in trading

  • Premium scaling: ITM options have intrinsic value; OTM options are pure time value. ITM premiums are HIGHER.
  • Probability of finishing ITM: Roughly equals the option's delta. A 0.30-delta call has ~30% chance of ending ITM.
  • Strategy choice:
    • Selling 2-3% OTM calls = aggressive premium (often gets challenged)
    • Selling 5-7% OTM calls = safer but tiny premium
    • Selling ATM calls = highest premium, highest chance of assignment
  • Roll triggers: "Spot crossed strike" → just went ITM → strategy decisions kick in.
💡 Why this matters Once you internalize ITM/ATM/OTM, you can read any option chain at a glance: the green calls are ITM (spot > K), the red ones are OTM (spot < K), and where the colors flip is where ATM lives. This is the foundation of every strategy in the rest of the book.
Chapter 3

Black-Scholes Pricing

The cornerstone equation. Five inputs in, one number out.
📖 What this chapter is about

The Black-Scholes equation is to options trading what F=ma is to mechanics. It says: given just five inputs — current price, strike, time, interest rate, and volatility — the fair price of a European option is uniquely determined.

By the end you'll: (a) know what every Nifty option on your screen should cost, (b) be able to identify when the market is over- or under-pricing options, (c) understand why "implied volatility" is the only mystery input — everything else is observable.

Black and Scholes (1973) proved that if you continuously rebalance a mix of stock and bonds, you can perfectly replicate any European option's payoff. The replicating portfolio's cost = the option's no-arbitrage price.

Chapter 4

The Greeks — Sensitivities

How option price changes when the world changes.
📖 What this chapter is about

Black-Scholes gives one number — the option's fair price right now. The Greeks tell you how that price will change when the underlying moves, time passes, or volatility shifts. Each Greek is a partial derivative of the BS formula with respect to one input.

By the end you'll: (a) read any option position as a portfolio of Δ, Γ, Θ, ν exposures, (b) know which Greeks make money for sellers vs. buyers, (c) understand why the system uses delta-based triggers (delta = the most reliable risk meter).

🔍 What is Delta really?
🚗

The simplest analogy: Delta is your option's "speed." If the underlying moves ₹1, the option moves ₹Δ. A 0.30 delta call moves 30 paise per ₹1 of spot move. A 0.80 delta call moves 80 paise.

🎯

Delta has THREE simultaneous meanings — every one is true:

  • Sensitivity: ₹X change in spot → ₹(X·Δ) change in option price.
  • Hedge ratio: To neutralize the option's directional risk, hold |Δ| shares against it.
  • Probability of ITM: Δ ≈ chance the option finishes in-the-money. A 0.30 delta = 30% probability.

📏 Range and what each value means

For calls, delta runs from 0 to 1. For puts, delta runs from −1 to 0 (mirror of calls). The absolute value tells the same story:

How professional traders use Delta

  • Sizing positions: "I want 30% of a Nifty share's exposure" → sell a 0.30 delta call.
  • Setting roll triggers: Delta ≥ 0.35 = roll-up threshold (the trade is becoming uncomfortable).
  • Building delta-neutral books: Long 100 deltas + short 100 deltas = market-neutral (income from theta only).

⚠️ Common pitfalls

  • Treating delta as constant: Delta itself changes (via gamma). A "small" position today can become a big one tomorrow.
  • Confusing delta with directional view: Selling a 0.30 call isn't "30% bullish" — it's "70% chance you keep the premium."
  • Ignoring near-expiry: Same 0.30 delta means very different risk on Day 30 vs Day 1.

🔍 What is Gamma really?
🏎️

If Delta is speed, Gamma is acceleration. It tells you how fast your speed (delta) is itself changing. A car can be slow (low Δ) but accelerating hard (high Γ) — about to become fast.

📊

  • At-the-money strikes — the bend is sharpest right at K.

  • High-IV environments reduce gamma slightly (uncertainty smooths the curve).

  • Selling ATM weekly options for "income": One bad day, the gamma blows up your premium income for the month.
  • Ignoring it during quiet markets: Gamma is invisible until volatility hits — then it's the dominant risk.

🔍 What is Theta really?
⏳

Theta is the price tag of holding an option as time passes. Every day, an option loses some value — even if nothing else in the market changes. Why? Because there's less time for the option to become valuable. Tomorrow's option is worth less than today's, all else equal.

💧

Picture a bucket of premium with a leak. The buyer paid for a full bucket; theta is the rate the water drains. For sellers, that drained water flows directly into your account. Day after day, drip after drip, no matter what spot does.

Theta is non-linear (this is critical)

  • 30+ days to expiry: Theta drips slowly. Daily decay is minimal.
  • 15-30 days: Decay accelerates. Most theta-harvesting strategies live here.
  • 5-15 days: Theta accelerates fast. Daily P&L for sellers is meaningful.
  • 0-5 days: Theta crashes the option's value. ATM options can lose 50% in 2 days.

This is why "sell at 30-45 DTE, close at 50% profit" is the industry-standard theta-harvest playbook.

Where theta is highest

  • At-the-money strikes — most uncertainty, most theta.
  • Short-dated options — accelerated decay near expiry.
  • High-IV environments — bigger premiums = bigger theta in absolute ₹.

🔍 What is Vega really?
🌊

Vega is the option's exposure to traders' opinions about future volatility. Spot can stay perfectly still and your option price can still move — because the MARKET'S BELIEF about future σ changed. Vega measures how sensitive your option is to this belief.

😱

Vega is the "fear-and-greed" Greek. When markets panic (VIX spikes), implied vol surges and ALL option prices rise — even if spot itself didn't move. Long options gain; short options lose. This is invisible damage that catches sellers off guard.

Where vega lives

  • At-the-money options have the highest vega.
  • Long-dated options have FAR more vega than short-dated. A 90-day ATM has ~3× the vega of a 30-day ATM.
  • Deep ITM/OTM have low vega — there's not much uncertainty about whether they'll be exercised.

  • Sell vega before catalysts: RBI meeting, earnings, budget — IV pumps. Selling premium when IV is rich, buying back after IV crushes.

  • "Long-vol" hedges: Buy long-dated puts as crash protection. They benefit from BOTH spot drops AND IV spikes.

⚠️ Common pitfalls

  • Selling options into a panic: Vega is at peak; you collect more premium but your downside is enormous if VIX spikes further.
  • Buying long-dated calls in low VIX: If IV expands later, your call gains; but if IV crushes, you lose even with spot up.
  • Ignoring vega on long-dated covered calls: A 6-month CC has 5× the vega of a monthly CC. IV spikes = your short calls lose value rapidly.

Equation 12.1 — Sharpe ratio

Sharpe = (Rstrategy − Rrisk-free) / σstrategy
@if (expandedVar() === '12.1') {
Variable definitions

📝 In plain English, this equation says

💡 Why this matters Returns alone are misleading. Sharpe = return per unit of risk. A strategy returning 12% with 4% volatility (Sharpe ≈ 1.0) is BETTER than 18% with 30% volatility (Sharpe ≈ 0.4). Sharpe = (9 − 7) / 12 = 0.17 — modest but positive.

Your job isn't to find the magic strategy. It's to understand which math is operating right now, and to choose the tool whose math matches the moment.

Exit & Rolling Strategy

Knowing when to act — and when to do nothing.

The 4 exit triggers

  1. Take-profit: Call price drops to ≤ 50% of entry premium → close, sell new cycle.
  2. Roll-up: Call delta ≥ 0.35 AND DTE > 5 → close + sell at higher strike.
  3. Assignment defense: DTE ≤ 1 AND ITM → close to avoid auto-assignment.
  4. Hedge roll: Hedge put has 30 DTE remaining → roll to new 90-day put.

Equation 13.7 — Roll cost-benefit

RollEV = (new premium) − (close cost) − friction
📝 In plain English

Only roll if the new premium meaningfully exceeds the close cost plus friction. Rolling for ₹500 when friction is ₹400 = wasted motion.

📝 Decision Rationale — Exit Triggers

Take-Profit (50% rule) — when to fire

Premium drops to ≤ 50% of entry → close. Why? Theta decay is non-linear. The first 50% of decay happens in the first ~⅓ of the option's life. Capturing it and redeploying earns more than waiting for the slow back half while exposing yourself to gamma reversal.

Roll-Up (delta 0.35) — when to fire

Call delta crosses 0.35 → roll to higher strike. Why 0.35? At delta 0.35, the call has crossed from "income tool" to "loss generator." Probability of finishing ITM has hit 35%. Rolling NOW (still OTM) is cheap; rolling AFTER spot crosses strike costs 4-5x more in intrinsic.

Assignment Defense (DTE ≤ 1 + ITM) — when to fire

Call is ITM with 1 day or less to expiry → close immediately. Why? Letting it auto-assign sells your NB at the strike — losing the rest of the cycle's potential. Closing manually is always cheaper than assignment costs.

Hedge Roll (30 DTE remaining) — when to fire

Long hedge put has 30 DTE remaining → roll to new 90-day put. Why 30 DTE? Below 30 DTE, gamma on the long put accelerates. Time decay also picks up. Rolling at 30 DTE preserves the put's effectiveness without paying for accelerating decay.

The priority hierarchy

When multiple stops fire simultaneously, the system follows priority: Stop 3 (Assignment) > Stop 2 (Roll-Up) > Stop 1 (Take-Profit) > Stop 4 (Hold). Higher priority wins. This prevents conflicting actions.
